A distributed system is a collection of autonomous computing nodes that communicate over a network and coordinate their actions to appear to the end user as a single, coherent system. Instead of relying on a single powerful machine, distributed architectures harness the combined processing power and storage of multiple servers to handle immense workloads, achieve high availability, and deliver fault tolerance.
Designing distributed systems introduces unique trade-offs, famously captured by the CAP theorem, which states that a distributed data store can simultaneously guarantee only two out of three properties: Consistency, Availability, and Partition Tolerance. Because network partitions are inevitable in real-world infrastructure, system architects must explicitly trade off strong data consistency against constant service availability. This requirement gives rise to key design concepts like eventual consistency, consensus algorithms such as Paxos and Raft, and distributed data replication strategies.
Managing latency, handling independent node failures, and achieving reliable state synchronization across geographical regions are among the primary engineering challenges in this paradigm. Despite this operational complexity, distributed systems form the backbone of modern technology. They power global cloud platforms, large-scale microservices, distributed databases, and high-throughput event streams, enabling applications to scale dynamically to millions of concurrent users worldwide.
